## Time and Space in Music

Here we see that a change has occurred. Generally speaking, arrangement is mostly about change. We change something, and that change somehow reflects on our piece.

### The Non-Linearity of Musical Time

And it is worth noting a very important point here, which is, well, obvious, but few people think about it: time in music is non-linear and not unidirectional, unlike in life. In life, we constantly move forward in time. In music, that is not the only way it works.

In music, relatively speaking, there are three simultaneous directions:
1. One direction is **in the moment**, when we hear the sound. What is sounding right now is the present in time.
2. Music also has a **past**. This is what we have already listened to. In this case, that is our ambient intro. It is what happened in the past.
3. And there is also the **future**. This is what we expect to hear based on what we've heard before and what we are hearing right now. That is, our listener expectation of what might happen next.

And music is constantly moving in all these directions. That is, its present is constantly changing in the sense that the sound is always shifting and going somewhere. We have a vector pointing toward the future. And what happens in the present affects how we perceive that future. And the past also changes. It's not just that we listened to the ambient intro and it stayed somewhere behind. It continues to be part of the new, constantly updating context. And accordingly, the track's past also changes as we listen to the track in the present, continually being supplemented by new contextual clarifications.

## The Main Principle: Music Must Be Grown, Not Built

And thus we move on to the final principle of building an arrangement for today, which is the following.

Very often you could listen, you could hear people say that a track needs to be built like a house, that we have a foundation, we have drums, we have a bass, then we have harmony, melody, and all of this is layered vertically, and we build this structure like a house. But that is only the external side. That is a one-sided view of arrangement.

When we take into account all these previous things I talked about, let's summarize:
- We are talking about the fact that arrangement is a form—or rather, a process of revealing content through form.
- We are talking about the connection between the vertical and the horizontal, about space and time being unified in music.
- We spoke about musical time being multidirectional. It works both forward and backward.
- And we say that music is born from contradictions, that arrangement is built out of how we structure our work with contradictions.

And the concluding part is that **music must be grown, not built**. And this is the main principle of arrangement. It must grow.

We took some fragment here, threw something together intuitively, and got some material, some mold. And then we work with it from the standpoint of growing a full-fledged tree of a musical organism out of this mold in time and space simultaneously, in the past and in the future simultaneously, playing with the listener's memory, with the listener's expectations.

And in this way, we approach arrangement not purely formally, not like craftsmen who know that "I work in such and such a genre, I need a bass like this here, at such and such a minute a hi-hat must appear, and something else." No, we don't start from some external qualities of the arrangement, or even random, arbitrary phenomena that have become entrenched in culture. We start from the internal logic of the material's own development, from internal dialectics, from working with contradictions. And thus our entire arrangement turns out to be cohesive while remaining internally diverse and rich.

### Dialectical Regularities and Aesthetic Pleasure

That is, our task as an arranger is to deepen contradictions in such a way that things are as lively as possible, as dramatic as possible in a sense, to clash these contradictions. But we must necessarily resolve these contradictions because that is what the listener will expect from us. Of course, we can play with this. We don't have to give the listener everything they expect, but by and large, we should give it to them.

And because this is the foundation from which a person derives aesthetic pleasure from music—from recognizing dialectical regularities in music, which are everywhere, everything that exists, everything that has being, is born from dialectical contradictions. And a person knows this. That is, they don't know it on a conceptual level, but it is natural and familiar to them. And, recognizing this dialectic in music, these contradictions, observing them and getting the expected result from this, a person experiences aesthetic pleasure.

In this regard, the difference between simple music and advanced, complex, academic music lies solely in how much craft, talent, and imagination the composer has to unfold this game of contradictions within their musical material without losing coherence along the way, so that we don't wander off into some contradictions brought in from the outside, but rather stem from the material we have.

We have a motif. That's it, contradictions must develop from this motif. We have a key. If we modulate to another key, it must be a transition prompted by contradictions within the key we were working in.

## Practical Recommendations and Conclusion

In this sense, my practical recommendation is that when you are working on an arrangement and hit a wall, you shouldn't be thinking: "Well, I should put a bass here because it's customary in the genre I work in" or "After the two-minute mark, something definitely needs to appear here," and so on. These are all externally non-essential thoughts. They can help you, but they can also distract you. They can help you choose the right instrument, or they can clutter the track with unnecessary fuss.

It is much more productive to ask yourself questions regarding contradictions:
- When I insert a new instrument: what contradiction does it set up? What contradiction does it resolve?
- Or perhaps it deepens a contradiction that was there before?
- Or, for example, you don't know where to move the track next. Look at what contradictions you have in the material you already have, in this loop you can't break out of: where can I develop them, and so on.

If you think specifically like a dialectician in your track, solutions will come to you... maximum organically and naturally for the material you are working with.

And that is my main message today: how to make an arrangement organic and cohesive, while at the same time preserving richness—or rather, conversely, making the arrangement rich and diverse while preserving cohesion.
